abstract
Suppose that one is given a Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) on a road network, but does not have access to
detailed information about that network. One could obtain a heuristic solution by solving a modified version of the problem, in which true road distances are replaced with planar Euclidean distances. We test
this heuristic, on two different types of VRP, using real road network data for twelve cities across the
world. We also give guidelines on the kind of VRP for which this heuristic can be expected to give good
results.

1. Introduction
Vehicle Routing Problems (VRPs) are an important family of combinatorial optimisation problems, and there is a huge literature on
them (see, e.g. the books Ball et al., 1995; Golden and Assad, 1988;
Golden et al., 2008; Toth and Vigo, 2014). In most existing VRP
models, the customers and depot(s) are represented by nodes in
a complete graph, and it is assumed that the distance between each
pair of nodes is known. In practice, however, VRPs are often
defined on road networks (e.g. Fleischmann, 1985; Letchford
et al., 2014; Orloff, 1974). We follow (Cornuéjols et al., 1985;
Letchford et al., 2013) in calling these Steiner VRPs.
If one has access to detailed road network data, it is usually possible to transform a Steiner VRP into a standard VRP, by solving a
series of shortest-path problems (see, e.g., Cornuéjols et al., 1985;
Fleischmann, 1985). When the road network is huge, however,
the shortest path computations themselves may consume a significant amount of time and memory. Moreover, for a given pair of
nodes, it may happen that the cheapest, shortest and quickest
paths are not the same. Thus, when a Steiner VRP involves more
than one feature (cost, distance and time, respectively), such a
transformation may not be possible. In that case, specialised
approaches are needed (e.g. Ben Ticha et al., 2018; Garaix et al.,
2010; Letchford et al., 2014).
A related issue is that some VRP heuristics are explicitly
designed to work on ‘‘planar Euclidean” instances, in which the
depot and customer nodes have known coordinates. Examples
include the classical ‘‘sweep” and ‘‘petal” heuristics (Foster and
Ryan, 1976; Gillett and Miller, 1974), and variations of them
(e.g., Renaud et al., 1996).
Another related stream of literature is concerned with the difference between distances in road networks and Euclidean distances (e.g. Ballou et al., 2002; Berens, 1988; Berens and Körling,
1985; Brimberg and Love, 1992; Cole and King, 1968; Love and
Morris, 1972; Love and Morris, 1979; Phibbs and Luft, 1995). It is
known that the distance between two random points in a road network is typically around 30% larger than the Euclidean distance
(Cole and King, 1968; Love and Morris, 1972), though this varies
from country to country (Ballou et al., 2002; Berens, 1988). On
the other hand, the correlation between true and Euclidean distances is typically very high, at over 0.98, for most cities and countries (Love and Morris, 1972; Phibbs and Luft, 1995). This suggests
that, for Steiner VRPs, Euclidean distances multiplied by 1.3 could
be a reasonable surrogate for true distances.
The above considerations suggest the following three-phase
heuristic approach to Steiner VRPs: (1) Create an approximation
of the given instance, by replacing true distances with Euclidean
distances multiplied by 1.3 (or some other suitable constant); (2)
Solve the approximated instance, either exactly or heuristically,
and (3) attempt to convert the solution into a feasible solution to
the original instance. A natural question is whether this heuristic
scheme can lead to solutions of reasonable quality in practice. To

2. Literature review
We now briefly review the relevant literature. We cover ‘‘arc
routing” problems in SubSection 2.1, VRPs in road networks in
SubSection 2.2, the planar Euclidean TSP in SubSection 2.3, and
studies of road distances in SubSection 2.4.
2.1. The Steiner TSP
In the Steiner TSP, we are given a connected undirected graph
G ¼ ðV; EÞ, a positive cost ce for each e 2 E, and a set VR # V of required vertices. The task is to find a minimum-cost closed walk that
visits each required vertex at least once. Edges may be traversed
more than once if desired.
Cornuéjols et al. (1985) defined the Steiner TSP and presented
some polyhedral results for it. In the same year, Fleischmann
(1985) proposed a cutting-plane algorithm for the Steiner TSP
and obtained encouraging results. Some additional computational
results are given in Corberán et al. (2001).
Interest in the Steiner TSP was revived by Letchford et al.
(2013), who explored several integer programming formulations.
Xia et al. (2016) proposed to solve the problem with branch
decomposition and dynamic programming instead. A specialised
branch-and-cut algorithm was given in Rodríguez–Pereira et al.
(2019). Álvarez-Miranda and Sinnl (2019) proposed instead to convert Steiner TSP instances to standard TSP instances, and then use a
state-of-the-art TSP solver like CONCORDE (Applegate et al., 2006).
2.2. Other Steiner VRPs
Apart from the Steiner TSP, the Steiner VRPs that have received
most attention are Arc Routing Problems (ARPs). An ARP is a VRP in
which demands are located along the edges or arcs of a network,
rather than at nodes. In the literature on ARPs, it is common to
model problems directly on road networks, rather than on a complete graph (see, e.g. the books Corberán and Laporte, 2014; Dror,
2000).
Outside the ARP literature, a key paper is Garaix et al. (2010).
They pointed out that, in a road network, the shortest and quickest
paths between two vertices may differ. This led them to propose a
specialised exact algorithm for the Steiner VRP with time windows,
based on a data structure called a multi-graph. Letchford et al.
(2014) proposed an alternative algorithm that works on the original road network rather than the multi-graph. However, Ben Ticha
et al. (2018) showed that, in practice, the multi-graph approach
usually works better.
Another work worth mentioning, which however was never
published, is Fleischmann (1982). Fleischmann defined a ‘‘Steiner”
version of the CVRP, in which a node in V is designated the depot,
and there is a fleet of identical vehicles, each of capacity Q, located
at the depot. He also discussed various integer programming formulations of the problem.
Finally, we mention that Letchford et al. (2013) presented integer programming formulations of some other Steiner VRPs, in addition to the Steiner TSP.
2.3. The planar Euclidean TSP
In the planar Euclidean TSP, we are given the coordinates of
some points in the plane, and the cost of travel between any two
points is equal to the Euclidean distance between them. The goal
is to find a minimum-cost tour that passes through each point
exactly once. The planar Euclidean TSP is a special case of the socalled metric TSP, in which the costs obey the triangle inequality.
Unfortunately, the planar Euclidean TSP is strongly NP-hard
(Garey et al., 1976). On the other hand, there is some evidence that
it is a ‘relatively easy’ special case of the TSP:
 The metric TSP is APX-hard (Papadimitriou and Yannakakis,
1993), but there is a polynomial-time approximation scheme
for the planar Euclidean TSP (Arora, 1998).
 The fastest known exact algorithm for the TSP takes O n22n 
time (Held and Karp, 1962), but the planar Euclidean TSP can
be solved in O 2 ffiffi

time (de Berg et al., 2018).
 Large-scale planar Euclidean instances can often be solved to
proven optimality in a reasonable amount of time by branchand-cut (Applegate et al., 2006; Padberg and Rinaldi, 1991).
2.4. Road distances versus Euclidean distances
Cole and King (1968) defined the ‘‘deviation factor” of a road network as the average, over all pairs of nodes, of the ratio between the
road distance and the Euclidean distance. They stated that, for most
cities and countries, the deviation factor ranges from 1.2 to 1.4.
Love and Morris (1972), Love and Morris (1979) proposed some
more complex functions, based on ‘
p norms, to estimate road distances from planar coordinates. Unfortunately, as pointed out by
Berens (1988), Berens and Körling (1985), the Love–Morris functions involve parameters that must be estimated, and the optimal
parameter values can vary significantly from city to city.
Brimberg and Love (1992) presented an alternative function,
based on a linear combination of ‘
1 and ‘
2 distances. Again, however, the best parameters vary from city to city.
Phibbs and Luft (1995) computed the correlation between road
distances and Euclidean distances for several cities, using real data.
The average correlation coefficient was remarkably high at 0.987.
Interestingly, however, this reduced to 0.826 when the data was
restricted to pairs of points that are no more than 15 miles apart
in terms of Euclidean distance.
More recently, Ballou et al. (2002) computed the deviation factor for several cities and countries across the world. They found
that it ranged from 1.2 to 1.6, with mountains and rivers being
the main cause of large values.

3.1. Data collection
The first step was to select twelve cities from across the world.
We selected London, Paris, Madrid, Barcelona, Moscow, Istanbul,
New York, Mexico City, Hanoi, Seoul, Karachi and Johannesburg.
Comprehensive data on the road networks of each of these cities
are available from OpenStreetMap (OpenStreetMap, 2004). Road
junctions and key landmarks are represented by nodes, and roads
(or road segments) are represented by edges. The position of each
node is given by its latitude and longitude, and the length of each
road (or road segment) is given in kilometres.
We used the Python package OSMnx (Boeing, 2017) to extract
and process data from OpenStreetMap. For each city, we selected
a key landmark as a ‘‘town centre”, and then computed the size of
the smallest square, with the given centre, that contained 2500
nodes. We then stored those nodes, together with all edges that
connected pairs of the selected nodes. For a given city, we let V
denote the set of 2500 nodes, E the set of edges and G the graph.
For reasons which will become clear, for each city, we also computed a smaller square, centred on the same point, that contained
only 2000 nodes.

3.4. Creation of Steiner capacitated VRPs
We also created 96 instances of the Steiner CVRP. To do this, we
simply took each of the Steiner TSP instances, set the demand of
each required node to one, and set the vehicle capacity Q to
jVRj=5. The node closest to the centre of the square was selected
to be the depot. The objective function is to minimise the total distance travelled, and there is no limit on the number of vehicles
used. (Of course, at least five vehicles are needed in any feasible
solution.).

4.2. The heuristic
The next step was to run the heuristic for each of the instances.
In more detail, we did the following for each instance:
1. Construct a planar Euclidean TSP instance with jVRj nodes, in
which the cost of travel between nodes u and v is dðu; vÞ. Then
feed that TSP instance into CONCORDE.
2. Store the optimal TSP tour and let L be its cost.
3. Select an arbitrary starting node, and traverse the tour. Let
vk be the kth node visited in the tour, where k ranges from
1 to jVRj.
4. For k ¼ 1; ... ; jVRj  1, run Dijkstra’s algorithm to compute a
shortest path in G from vk to vkþ1. Also compute a shortest path
from vjVRj to v1.
5. Replace each edge of the TSP tour with the corresponding shortest path, to obtain a heuristic solution to the original Steiner TSP
instance.
6. Let U be the length of the heuristic solution, i.e., the sum of the
lengths of the jVRj shortest paths.
We now make three remarks about this procedure:
 The shortest-path phase in step 3 takes very little time in practice, since vk tends to be very close to vkþ1 in G, and we abort
the Dijkstra call as soon as the distance label for vkþ1 becomes
permanent.
 Let tðeÞ denote the number of times that edge e is traversed in
the heuristic solution. If tðeÞ > 2, the solution can be improved
as follows: if tðeÞ is even, set tðeÞ to 2, otherwise, set it to 1.
We let U denote the cost of the improved solution.

5.1. Heuristics
Since current exact CVRP algorithms tend to struggle when
instances have more than 200 customers (Pecin et al., 2017), we
used heuristics both for the Steiner CVRP and for the Euclidean
approximation. To make the comparison fair, we used exactly the
same heuristic for both variants. In particular, we used a
route-first cluster-second heuristic (see Beasley (1983), Bodin
(1975)). In our experience, this kind of heuristic gives a good
balance between solution quality and running time, while being
easy to implement.
First we explain how the heuristic works when Euclidean
approximation is not used. The first step is to solve (optimally)
the Steiner TSP on the set VR, using the same method that we used
in SubSection 4.1. This yields a ‘‘giant tour” that passes through all
nodes in VR. The next step is to run Dijkstra’s algorithm one more
time, to compute shortest paths from the depot to each node in VR.
Now, let vk be the kth required node visited in the giant tour,
where k ranges from 1 to jVRj. For each pair 1 6 i < j 6 m, we create a trip that travels from the depot to vi via a shortest path, then
travels to viþ1; ... ; viþj, and then returns to the depot. If the trip is
feasible, we store it in memory. We then use the standard method
(see again Beasley, 1983) to find the best CVRP solution that uses
only trips that are in memory. Apart from the solution of the TSP
in CONCORDE, the whole approach takes Oð Þ jVjjVRj log jVj time.
We let T be the total running time, and U denote the resulting
upper bound.

gives a ‘‘giant tour” on VR. We then use the standard method to

to convert the CVRP solution into a Steiner CVRP solution. Apart

6. Concluding remarks
We have seen that, on the whole, using Euclidean distances
instead of real road distances gives acceptable results for the Steiner TSP and Steiner CVRP. This is especially true when only a small
proportion of nodes require service, which is the case in almost all
real-life applications.
We believe that Euclidean approximation would also work well
for some more complex Steiner VRPs, for example with split deliveries, backhauls, multiple depots, and/or demands located on edges
as well as nodes. We are currently working on the use of Euclidean
approximation to compute fast upper bounds for arc routing
problems.
